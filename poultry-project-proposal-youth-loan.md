## 1. Executive Summary

This proposal seeks funding to start a small-scale broiler chicken production business, beginning
with a first batch of **100 day-old broiler chicks**. Broiler production was chosen because it has
a short production cycle (6–8 weeks from chick to sale), strong and steady local demand for
chicken meat, and a proven, well-understood production process that a first-time farmer can manage
successfully with basic training and support.

The total capital required to fund the first production cycle — chicks, feed, vaccines/medication,
extending the applicant's existing structure, and basic equipment — is estimated at
**US$464.00** (see Section 6 for the full itemized breakdown). This amount is requested as a loan
under the Youth Loan scheme. Based on realistic local selling prices,
the first batch is projected to generate enough revenue to repay the loan and reinvest in a second,
larger batch — building toward a sustainable, self-financing poultry business.

## 2. Background / About the Applicant

I am a young resident of Luveve who has always been drawn to poultry farming as a realistic way to
build my own income rather than wait indefinitely for formal employment. I already have a small
structure at home that can house between 50 and 100 chicks, which I set up myself with limited
resources — this loan will let me extend that structure and add separate compartments so I can
rotate batches, keeping incoming day-old chicks apart from an older, near-market-ready batch. That
way I can sell one batch and start the next immediately, without any downtime and without risking
young chicks by mixing them with older birds. Poultry farming appeals to me because the short
production cycle means I can see real results and start repaying quickly, and because it lets me
serve my own community directly — supplying fresh, affordable chicken to my neighbours here in
Luveve. My family also has a rural home about 60km from Bulawayo, which gives me a natural second
site to expand production and sales to once the Luveve operation is established, and I plan to use
solar power to run the project reliably at both sites without depending on an unstable grid supply.
As the business grows, I also want to create an opportunity to bring other young people from the
area on board.

## 3. Business Description

**Nature of business:** Small-scale commercial broiler chicken production for sale of live birds
to local households, vendors, and butcheries.

**Objectives:**
1. Successfully raise and sell the first batch of 100 broilers within 8 weeks of receiving chicks.
2. Use profit from the first batch to part-repay the loan and fund a second, larger batch
   (targeting 150–200 birds) within 3 months.
3. Establish a reliable local customer base (repeat household buyers, at least one local
   butchery/vendor relationship) within the first two production cycles.
4. Grow into a stable, income-generating small enterprise providing full-time or significant
   supplementary income within 12 months.
5. Expand production to a family rural home approximately 60km from Bulawayo as a second site,
   once the Luveve operation is established, opening a second market and spreading production
   across two locations.

**Why broilers, specifically:**
- Short cycle (6–8 weeks) means faster return on investment and loan repayment compared to layers
  (eggs) or crop farming, which take much longer to generate income.
- Chicken meat has consistent, year-round local demand — not a seasonal product.
- Production knowledge is widely available locally (agricultural extension officers, established
  local broiler farmers, input suppliers who provide guidance to new farmers).

## 4. Market Analysis

**Target customers:**
- Households in [applicant's area, e.g. Luveve and surrounding suburbs] buying live birds directly,
  especially around month-end and festive periods when demand rises.
- Local vendors/tuck-shops who resell chicken.
- At least one local butchery approached for a standing supply arrangement once volumes grow.
- A second customer base at the family's rural home approximately 60km from Bulawayo, once
  operations expand to that site (Section 3, Objective 5).

**Demand:** Chicken is the most affordable and widely consumed meat in Zimbabwe. Local demand
consistently outstrips what small-scale producers in most high-density suburbs can supply — most
broilers sold informally in areas like Luveve are sold well before maturity through word-of-mouth
pre-orders.

**Pricing:** Live broilers currently sell in the Bulawayo informal/retail market at approximately
**US$6–8 per bird** (roughly US$3.50–4.50 per kg live weight), depending on size and season. This
proposal uses a conservative estimate of **US$7 per bird** in its projections (Section 7).

**Competition:** A small number of other backyard/small-scale producers exist in the area, but
demand is not fully met — new entrants selling healthy, well-raised birds at a fair price are able
to find buyers, particularly through direct household sales and pre-orders rather than competing on
price alone.

## 5. Operations Plan

**Production cycle (per batch, ~8 weeks):**

| Week | Activity |
|---|---|
| Week 0 | Receive 100 day-old chicks; brooding house prepared and pre-warmed |
| Weeks 1–2 | Brooding stage — heat lamp/brooder maintained, starter mash feed, first vaccinations (Newcastle disease, Gumboro per standard schedule) |
| Weeks 3–5 | Grower stage — grower mash, reduced heating, regular health monitoring |
| Weeks 6–8 | Finisher stage — finisher mash, birds reach market weight (~1.8–2.2kg) |
| Week 8 | Sale of birds; house cleaned, rested, and disinfected before next batch |

**Housing:** An existing small structure at the applicant's home, currently sized for 50–100
chicks, will be **extended and divided into two compartments** — one for an incoming batch of
day-old chicks, one for the current batch as it grows toward market weight. This rotation design
means a new batch can start the moment the previous one is sold, without a gap in production and
without mixing different-age birds (which raises disease risk). Adequate ventilation, dry bedding
(wood shavings/sawdust), and secure fencing against predators and theft will be maintained
throughout.

**Power supply:** Solar power will be used to run the brooding heat lamp and lighting, keeping the
project running reliably during grid outages/load-shedding — brooding-stage chicks are especially
vulnerable to losing heat overnight, so this removes a real risk to the whole batch. The same solar
setup is intended to support the rural home site once operations expand there (Section 3,
Objective 5).

**Inputs required:** day-old broiler chicks, starter/grower/finisher stock feed, Newcastle
disease and Gumboro vaccines, basic vitamins/electrolytes, feeders and drinkers, a heat source for
brooding, and wood-shaving litter. Full itemized costs are in Section 6.

**Biosecurity & disease management:** Vaccination schedule followed per supplier/extension officer
guidance; footbath at house entrance; visitors limited during brooding stage; sick birds isolated
immediately; advice sought from the nearest agricultural extension officer or input supplier at the
first sign of illness.

| | |
|---|---|
| Chicks purchased | 100 |

| Less: total production cost (Section 6) | US$464.00 |
| **Projected net profit, first batch** | **≈ US$166.00** |

Profit is modest on the first batch — expected, since it also covers the fixed one-off cost of the
structure extension and equipment (feeders, drinkers, heat lamp) that will be reused for every
future batch at no extra cost. **From the second batch onward**, with the compartments and
equipment already in place, the same 100-bird batch would cost roughly **US$260** (chicks, feed,
vaccines, vitamins, litter, transport — no further housing or equipment spend) against the same
~US$630 revenue — a projected profit of **≈ US$370 per cycle**. Because the new compartment design
lets a fresh batch start the moment the current one is sold, this US$370 profit is realistically
earned roughly every 8 weeks on an ongoing basis, not just once.

## 9. Risks and Mitigation

| Risk | Mitigation |
|---|---|
| Disease outbreak (e.g. Newcastle disease) | Strict vaccination schedule, biosecurity measures (footbath, visitor limits, isolation of sick birds), guidance from local extension officer |
| High mortality in brooding stage | Reliable heat source maintained, chicks sourced from a reputable hatchery, close monitoring in first two weeks |
| Feed price increases | Feed costed conservatively above; will compare prices across at least two local suppliers before purchase |
| Slow sales / birds not sold at target price | Pre-orders taken from neighbours/household contacts before birds reach maturity; flexible pricing for bulk/early buyers |
| Theft / predators | Secure fencing and a lockable structure; house checked daily |
| Water shortage | [Confirm applicant's water access — borehole, municipal supply, well — and note backup plan if supply is unreliable] |
| Power outages / load-shedding | Solar power used for the brooding heat lamp and lighting, reducing dependence on an unstable grid supply |
